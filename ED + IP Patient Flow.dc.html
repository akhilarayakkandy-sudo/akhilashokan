Case Study · Healthcare · Enterprise EHR

Designing Patient Flow Across Emergency and Inpatient Care

I helped define and design connected ED and IP tracker boards inside an existing EHR, making complex patient operations easier to manage while reusing existing clinical modules.

Role

UX/Product Designer

Contribution

Requirements, workflow definition, UI/UX design

Product

BlueEHR, BlueBriX Health

Client

Hospital Metropolitano

Status

Both boards live in production

01 — The problem

One patient, two very different workflows

ED and IP each involve many activities, patient states, clinical tasks and administrative actions. For the patient they are often one stay: assessed and treated in ED, then admitted, moved between care settings and discharged.

Before this work, inpatient flow was handled outside the EHR. Spreadsheets tracked bed status, external applications handled admission queues, and transfer decisions happened in person or over the phone.

Emergency Department

Patient registration and encounter creation Triage, vitals and chief complaint Prescribing medication, starting IVs, ordering labs Placing patients in a bed, recliner or the waiting area Deciding the next step: discharge or IP admission

Inpatient

Admission waiting list Admission and bed assignment Moves between Hospitalization, ICU, Maternity and Neonatal Transfer, OR and discharge queues Discharge

The challenge was to understand these workflows and design tracker boards that supported the required operations, while working inside an EHR that already had its own modules and patterns.

02 — Patient journey

Mapping where patients come from and where they go

Patients reach inpatient care from four places: OP, ED, OR, or a direct admission. From there they can move between IP care settings, go to OR and come back, before discharge.

Not every patient follows the same path. The map below shows the main routes we worked through.

State and location are separate

Mapping the journey showed that a patient's state (waiting, admitted, queued for transfer) is independent of their care setting. A patient can be "Queued for Transfer" while still in a Hospitalization bed. The boards had to show both clearly.

Maternity added another layer. A delivery creates a newborn record that can move to Neonatal on its own and later rejoin the mother.

03 — ED workflow

The Emergency Department

In ED, many clinical and administrative tasks happen around the same time. Staff assess the patient, record vitals and the chief complaint, prescribe medication, start IVs and decide the next step in care. Registration and encounter creation also need to happen.

The ED board organises patients by the stage they are at:

Waiting Room → ED Patients → Queue for Discharge or Queue for IP Admission

What each ED patient row carries

Location

Bed, recliner or lobby, changeable from the row.

Patient and alerts

Name, MRN, age, plus allergy and fall-risk flags.

Triage and time in dept

Critical, Moderate or Stable. Long stays are highlighted.

Chief complaint and key vital

The vital that needs attention, not every reading.

Clinical tasks open from quick actions on each row. Pending work across patients collects in a notification area beside the list. Both are covered under the challenges below.

04 — IP workflow

Inpatient care

Patients enter the admission waiting list from ED, the front desk, OR, or as a direct or internal admission. Each one then moves through a sequence of states:

Waiting → IP Admission → Admitted → Queued for Transfer / OR / Discharge → Discharged

At the same time, an admitted patient sits in one of four care settings (Hospitalization, ICU, Maternity, Neonatal) and can be transferred between them.

Who uses the IP board

Nurses

Monitor assigned patients, check bed status, track care transitions, update status.

Front desk / admissions

Process admissions, assign beds, monitor the admission queue.

Hospital administrators

Monitor census, find bottlenecks, manage transfers and discharges.

Working it out on the whiteboard

The IP structure was sketched with the team: the four care settings, the mother-and-baby case, the queue tabs (Q.F IP Admission, IP Admission, Q.F Discharge, Q.F OR), and open questions such as how to count neonatal beds and whether Neonatal needed its own board. The answer was a single IP board covering all four settings.

05 — Challenges

Three challenges that shaped the work

01

Understanding and mapping the ED workflow

Challenge

ED care includes many clinical and administrative activities that overlap. I needed to understand how they fit together and how the application should support them.

My contribution

I contributed to the requirements and workflow definition with the BA, working through the clinical steps, administrative tasks, patient movement, and the information the board needed to show.

Design response

The board follows the stages of ED care (waiting, in treatment, queued for discharge or admission), and each row carries what staff check most: location, triage, time in department, chief complaint and the key vital.

Why it mattered

The structure of the board came from the workflow, so where a patient sits on the board tells staff what happens next.

02

Making important notifications easier to find

1 · Initial idea

Show notifications inside each patient row.

2 · Feedback

The BA found they were not prominent enough and could be hard to find.

3 · Design change

Add a dedicated notification area to the board.

4 · Final approach

A Notification Center beside the patient list, grouped by type with counts.

In the final ED design, pending items are grouped by type: eMAR approvals, medications, lab orders, imaging, mobility, diet, safety precautions, lines and drains, wound care and other orders. Each group shows a count and expands to list the patients and their beds.

Why it mattered: important tasks are visible without staff having to spot a small detail inside one row among many.

03

Connecting new workflows with the existing EHR

Challenge

The boards were new, but BlueEHR already had established modules and workflows. They needed to support clinical operations without duplicating what existed.

My contribution

I designed how users reach existing modules from the boards: prescriptions, lab ordering, Cardex, input/output charts and other encounter forms.

Design response

Quick actions on each patient row open those modules in context. We also designed and implemented a single encounter that continues from ED into IP until discharge.

Why it mattered

Staff keep using modules they already know, and an admitted ED patient continues on the same encounter instead of starting a new one.

06 — Exploration and decisions

From dashboard to operational workspace

Early exploration started with a dashboard of charts, metrics and status groups. Stakeholder feedback showed that admissions staff needed to assign beds and manage queues from the same view. The board became a workspace where actions happen in the patient list.

Three iterations of the IP Admissions list

A smaller evolution: resource availability

The first summary widgets showed patient counts, the care team and doctors on duty.

When assigning beds and chairs, users also needed to see what was free. An availability summary was added: beds, recliners and the waiting area in ED, and beds per care setting in IP.

Staff could then see both the patient situation and the available resources when placing patients.

Decisions that shaped both boards

01An operational workspace, not just a dashboard

Staff need to act on patients, not only read summary numbers, so actions live in the list.

02Stages as tabs, with state and care setting kept apart

Queue tabs show where each patient is in the flow. In IP, care-setting sub-tabs filter by location without losing the state.

03Compact rows, with more detail on demand

Showing every field made rows cluttered. Core details stay visible and secondary information opens when needed. Trade-off: some detail is one step away, in exchange for a list staff can scan.

04A separate notification area

Pending work across patients is collected in one place rather than hidden in rows (challenge 02).

05Availability in the overview

Placement decisions need resource information next to the patient numbers.

06Reuse existing modules and interaction patterns

The boards use BlueEHR's existing buttons, dropdowns, modals and icon actions, and link to existing modules. New patterns were limited to what the boards needed, which kept training effort low.

07One encounter from ED through IP to discharge

The patient's stay is treated as one continuous encounter rather than separate ED and IP records.

09 — Reflection

What this project taught me

Map the workflow before the screen

The board structure came from the journey map and the queue model. In IP, transitions such as admitted to queued for transfer to discharge weren't fully modelled until development. Next time I would treat the state model as a design artefact from the start.

Design inside the system that exists

The useful work was often in how the boards reached existing modules, and in the single encounter, rather than in new UI.

Visibility needs its own place

Putting notifications inside rows seemed efficient, but the BA's review showed they got lost. Dense boards need a clear split between scanning the list and acting on it. Testing with realistic patient numbers made this obvious: scanning 50 patients is different from scanning 5.

Reviews made the design better

BA validation, developer feasibility checks and stakeholder demos each changed the design. The notification area and the move from dashboard to workspace both came from that feedback.
