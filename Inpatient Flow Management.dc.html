Inpatient Flow
Management

Designing a unified inpatient experience for complex hospital operations

Hospitals manage inpatients across multiple care settings—Hospitalization, ICU, Maternity, Neonatology—with fragmented visibility. Clinicians and administrators lack a single workspace to see patient flow, bed availability, care transitions, and critical actions. This case study documents the design of a dedicated inpatient tracking capability within BlueEHR, bringing unified operational oversight to complex hospital environments.

Context & Problem

Why This Capability Mattered

Managing inpatient flow requires understanding where patients are, what they need, and how to move them across care settings efficiently. Before this capability existed, hospital teams relied on external tools, manual coordination, and incomplete visibility into inpatient operations.

The Before & After Model

Before

Inpatient workflows were distributed across systems and tools—spreadsheets tracked bed status, external applications handled admission queues, transfer decisions happened in person or over the phone.

After

A dedicated inpatient capability built directly into BlueEHR unified operational oversight, patient visibility, and care-setting coordination in one workspace.

Core Complexity

Multiple Care Settings

Patients move across Hospitalization, ICU, Maternity, and Neonatology—each with different workflows, urgency patterns, and constraints.

State + Location Separation

A patient's status (Waiting, Admitted, Queued for Transfer) is independent from their care setting. Both dimensions matter.

Operational Density

Clinicians and administrators need deep visibility into multiple patients, beds, and workflows without context-switching between screens.

Maternity Complexity

Maternity patients may spawn newborn records in Neonatology, requiring tracking of both mother and child across separate care settings.

Primary UX Challenge

How to present a complex, multi-dimensional operational workspace that remains scannable, actionable, and aligned with clinical and administrative workflows—without requiring extensive training or documentation.

Key Constraints

Content Density

Hospital teams expect high information density. Making the board simple can sacrifice operational usefulness.

Familiar Interaction Language

Users already work in BlueEHR. The new capability must use existing interaction patterns.

Real-Time Coordination

Bed management and transfers happen quickly. Actions must be efficient and immediately visible.

Clinical Accountability

Every patient action is clinically significant. The UI must reduce errors and support clear decision-making.

Key Insight

Patient state and care setting are separate dimensions. A patient can be "Admitted to ICU" or "Queued for Transfer while in Hospitalization." The board must surface both clearly.

Two Examples

Scenario 1: Admitted + ICU

Patient moved from Hospitalization to ICU. They are now "Admitted" and live in "ICU" workspace. All ICU actions apply.

Scenario 2: Queued for Transfer + Hospitalization

Patient is "Queued for Transfer" but still assigned to a Hospitalization bed. Admissions must confirm the transfer destination before moving them.

Exploration & Iteration

From Dashboard Concept to Operational Workspace

Early exploration centered on a traditional dashboard model—charts, metrics, aggregate status. The team quickly realized clinicians and administrators needed transactional control, not just visibility. The board evolved into an operational workspace where actions happen directly within the context of the patient list.

Early Exploration: Dashboard → Workspace

Initial Direction (Figma Exploration)

Started with a card-based dashboard showing high-level metrics and status groups. Stakeholder feedback revealed this lacked actionability—admissions staff needed to assign beds and manage queues from the same view.

Early Figma exploration — dashboard card layout

Wireframe Refinement

Shifted to a table-based operational view. Patients listed by care setting, with status and actions visible inline. Bed inventory shown as a separate panel. This model aligned with clinical workflows and allowed faster decision-making.

Wireframe exploration — table-based layout with care-setting tabs

Patient Row Design Iterations

The individual patient row needed to balance compactness with actionability. Early iterations showed all fields at once; later versions collapsed secondary information behind expandable states.

Design principle: Keep rows compact by default (scannable), but make them expandable to show full context (allergies, medications, care setting history) when needed.

Care-Setting Movement Model

Exploration revealed that patients regularly move between settings. The board needed to show current setting clearly and make transfers effortless.

Exploration diagram — patient flow between care settings

This exploration helped the team understand that the board itself wasn't a destination—it was a launchpad for workflows. Admissions, bed assignment, transfers, and discharges all originated from the board view.

Decisions & Rationale

Design Decisions

Three core decisions shaped the final experience. Each addressed a specific tension between simplicity and operational reality.

01

Turn a Complex Workflow into a State-Based Board

Inpatient flow involves multiple simultaneous activities: admissions, bed assignments, transfers, discharges, care-setting changes. Rather than create separate screens for each, the team opted for a single operational board that surfaces all patient states in one view. Tabs by care setting (Hospitalization, ICU, Maternity, Neonatology) let users filter without losing context.

Benefit: Administrators see the full census at a glance. Nurses filter to their care setting. Admissions staff can search across all settings to find beds or coordinate transfers.

02

Keep the Patient Row Compact, But Make It Actionable

Early designs tried to show all patient data in a single row—medications, allergies, vitals, bed number, status, actions. This created visual clutter. The solution was a collapsible row structure: core information (name, bed, status) visible by default; secondary data (medications, allergies, care history) expandable on demand. Actions remain always-visible as an icon row.

Benefit: The board stays scannable (dozens of patients at once). Clinicians can quickly expand a specific row to get full context before taking action. No modal dialogs required for basic information lookup.

03

Reuse the EHR Interaction Language

Rather than invent new interaction patterns, the IP Tracker adopted BlueEHR's existing language: buttons, dropdowns, modals, icon actions. This reduced cognitive load for users who already work in the platform daily. New patterns (care-setting tabs, state-based filtering) were introduced thoughtfully, but core interactions felt familiar.

Benefit: Shorter onboarding. Reduced training effort. Consistency across the BlueEHR ecosystem. Users trust the familiar UI patterns to do what they expect.

Solution & Implementation

The Final IP Experience

A unified operational workspace for monitoring inpatient flow, coordinating care settings and accessing critical patient actions from one place.

Complete IP Tracker Board — patient list, availability panel, code team assignments, order approvals

The IP Tracker Board brings together patient census, bed availability, care-team coordination, and approval workflows in a single, dense operational view.

Core Features at a Glance

01

Operational Overview

Summary cards show total census by care setting, bed availability by urgency level (Severe, Medium, Low), and a snapshot of assigned care team. Administrators see hospital-wide status at a glance.

02

Expandable Patient Context

Each patient row shows name, bed, diagnosis, and vitals. Click to expand for full details: medications, allergies, admission history, assigned provider. Secondary data stays accessible but hidden by default.

03

Patient-Level Actions

An icon row on each patient enables transfers, discharges, status updates, order placement, and assignment changes. Actions are contextual—a patient queued for discharge shows different options than one newly admitted.

04

Care-Setting Tabs & Filtering

Users filter by care setting (All, ICU, Hospitalization, Maternity, Neonatology) or by status (Waiting, IP Admissions, Queued for Transfer, Queued for Discharge). Multiple filters can combine.

Reflection & Learnings

Reflection

Three lessons from this project inform how I approach complex healthcare product design.

01

Define Success Earlier

The project benefited from clarity on what the IP Tracker would and wouldn't do. Early discussions about "operational workspace, not diagnostic tool" prevented scope creep and kept design focused. Future projects should lock in a crisp definition of success before design begins.

02

Validate at Realistic Scale

Testing the board with real data (dozens of patients, multiple care settings, live bed inventory) revealed issues that small prototypes wouldn't show. Scanning 50 patients on a board is cognitively different from scanning 5. Layout, color, and action prominence matter much more at scale.

03

Model Transitions Earlier

Transitions—admission → assigned → queued for transfer → discharge—weren't deeply modeled until development. Earlier state-machine clarity would have prevented design ambiguities and developer back-and-forth. Future projects should diagram state models as a design artifact, not a dev deliverable.

In complex healthcare products, the workflow model often matters more than the individual screen.

The IP Tracker's success rested not on visual novelty but on a clear, honest model of how patients move through care settings and what information clinicians and administrators need to manage that flow. A beautiful screen that misunderstands the workflow is worse than a dense screen that gets it right.
