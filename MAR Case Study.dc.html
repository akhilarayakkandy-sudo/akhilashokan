Case Study · Healthcare / EHR

MAR
Medication Administration Record

Designing a time-based medication administration workflow for Emergency and Inpatient care

Role

UX Designer

Product

BlueEHR

Client

Hospital Metropolitano

Primary users

Nurses, Physicians, Pharmacists

Status

Live in production

Medication administration is a time-sensitive part of clinical care. Nurses need to know what medication is due, when it is due, what has already been given, and what still needs attention.

Before MAR, Hospital Metropolitano was using Excel for medication administration records. The requirement was to move this into a structured and easier-to-use experience inside BlueEHR.

I designed the MAR experience first for the Emergency Department. The MAR framework was later extended to support Inpatient workflows and connected to the ED, IP, and OR tracker boards.

The final MAR is now live at Hospital Metropolitano.

Core design idea: Make time the main structure of the medication workflow, while keeping different medication behaviors easy to find and understand.

02 — The problem

The challenge was not simply to replace an Excel sheet.

The medication workflow had many different behaviors:

Scheduled medications Due medications Overdue medications PRN medications Continuous medications Controlled substances Administered medications Refused / Not Administered medications Discontinued medications Completed medication courses

There were also dependencies outside MAR.

A medication could move through:

Prescription → Pharmacy review → Availability → MAR → Administration → Billing / Inventory

A pharmacy rejection could stop a medication from being administered. An administration record could affect billing and inventory. Providers also needed the patient's medication history across ED, IP, and OR.

This meant MAR could not be designed as an isolated screen.

03 — Research

Understanding the real workflow

The main source for understanding the clinical workflow was the client's UAT scripts. These contained real medication-administration scenarios used by the client team and helped me understand how nurses would work with the system.

The design process was

UAT scenarios → UX exploration → Self-review → Manager review → BA review → Developer feasibility check → Stakeholder review → Client review

I also reviewed existing BlueEHR patterns, including the calendar experience, and explored input from the BA and client team.

This was not a formal nurse research study. The UAT scenarios were the main source used to understand the workflow and edge cases.

Point-of-care constraint

The client expected nurses to potentially use laptops on wheels while preparing and administering medication.

The nurse needs to verify the correct:

01

Patient

02

Medication

03

Route

04

Dose

05

Time

The MAR therefore needed to support quick access to the right information without making the nurse move through several screens.

04 — Structure

From a list to a timeline

The initial direction was a list-based structure with tabs.

As I worked through the medication scenarios, I found a problem with a simple list: it showed the medications, but the relationship between the medication and its scheduled time was not strong enough.

I explored a time-based structure instead.

Why a horizontal timeline?

A vertical list uses the screen height to show medications. As more medications are added, the user needs to scroll through the list while also trying to understand when each medication belongs.

The horizontal timeline allowed time to become the main structure.

Time → Medication → Current attention

The MAR shows 24-hour time slots and a current-time indicator. Medication cards appear against their scheduled time, making it easier to understand the frequency and timing of each medication.

This approach was reviewed with the team and accepted by the client.

05 — Hierarchy

Designing the medication hierarchy

The biggest design challenge was not fitting the medication cards on the screen. It was deciding how much attention each medication should receive.

Every piece of clinical information can be important. I did not want to remove information just to make the screen look simpler. Instead, I used visual hierarchy to help users understand what needs attention first.

Higher attention

Due / Overdue

These medications need an action or follow-up, so they receive stronger visual emphasis.

Lower visual weight

Administered

Once a medication has been administered, it no longer needs the same level of attention. It remains available because users may need to review its administration details later.

Different behavior

PRN / Continuous

These medications do not always follow the same time-based behavior as scheduled medications, so they need a different access pattern.

Special identification

Controlled substances

Controlled medications need to be clearly identified. Instead of depending only on color, I used an icon so the information could be recognized without relying on color alone.

The result was a screen where medication cards could carry different levels of attention without every card competing equally.

06

PRN and Continuous medications

PRN and Continuous medications introduced a different problem. The challenge was not simply attention. It was accessibility.

A PRN medication may be needed when required rather than at one fixed time. A Continuous medication can remain active for a period of time.

If these medications were placed only inside the scrolling timeline, users could have to search for them when they needed them.

I therefore kept them in a fixed area below the main timeline. This created two simple interaction patterns:

Scheduled medications: follow the time-based timeline.

PRN / Continuous medications: remain easily accessible.

07

Medication states and sections

The MAR was organized into separate views so users could focus on the type of medication information they needed.

All

Shows the medication records across the main MAR workflow.

PRN

Shows medications that can be administered as needed.

Continuous

Shows medications that continue over a period of time.

Overdue

Shows medications that passed their allowed administration window without being documented.

Discontinued / Completed

Shows medications that have been intentionally discontinued or have completed their course.

A medication can therefore move through different states without losing its administration history.

08

Handling overdue medications

Overdue medication required special treatment.

The system still allows an overdue medication to be administered. The nurse records the actual administration time, while the system also keeps the system-tracked time.

This means the design needs to communicate two things:

The medication was due earlier → The medication may still need action now

I gave overdue medications stronger visual attention because they represent an unresolved scheduled action.

This also matters beyond the MAR screen. Administration records become part of the patient's treatment history and can help providers understand whether a medication was given and how the patient's condition changed over time.

09 — Administration

Administration without losing context

Once a nurse selects a medication, the administration action opens in a right-side panel rather than taking the user away from the MAR.

The panel keeps the medication context visible while allowing the user to complete the required documentation. The flow is:

Medication card → Administer → Review details → Enter administration information → Save

The administration panel includes

Medication details Frequency Route / administration information Pharmacist comments Additional instructions Administered by Documented by Administration status Administration date and time

10

Controlled substances and accountability

Controlled substances needed a different level of verification. The person administering the medication and the person verifying it must be different users.

The workflow captures:

Administered by → Amount wasted → Verified by → Verification

The verifier confirms the transaction using their credentials.

This was designed as a separate part of the administration flow rather than making every medication card carry all of these fields.

That kept the normal medication workflow simpler while still supporting the additional controlled-substance process.

11 — System effects

One action, multiple system effects

One of the most important things I learned from this project was that an action in MAR does not stay inside MAR.

For example

Medication administered

→ Administration record is saved

→ Billing can be triggered

→ Inventory can be reduced

→ Medication history is updated

→ Provider can review the administration record

If the pharmacy rejects a medication, the nurse should not be left with an unclear medication state. The provider may need to modify the prescription and send it again.

This is why MAR needed to be available from the:

ED Tracker Board → Inpatient Board → OR Board

The provider and care team need access to the patient's medication administration record across these workflows.

12

Designing for a connected EHR

The hardest part of this project was not designing one screen. Every decision affected another part of the system. For example:

Medication ordering affects MAR.

Pharmacy approval affects whether a medication can move forward.

MAR administration affects billing.

Administration affects inventory.

Medication status affects tracker-board visibility.

Administration history supports provider review.

Controlled-substance verification adds another user and permission step.

This made system knowledge very important.

For this project, understanding how BlueEHR works as a whole was just as important as designing the individual MAR screen.

13 — Exploration

What I explored

Before settling on the final interaction model, I explored:

List-based medication layouts Tab-based organization Existing BlueEHR calendar patterns Existing product patterns Client/UAT scenarios BA suggestions Client feedback

The list approach was useful as a starting point, but it did not communicate medication timing strongly enough.

The timeline became the stronger structure because it connected the medication directly to its scheduled time.

14 — Delivery

Review and implementation

My review process included:

UX self-review → Manager → BA → Developer feasibility → Stakeholder → Client

Not every feature went through a separate stakeholder review. After the BA was aligned with the approach, the design was demonstrated to stakeholders and then submitted to the client as required.

After the design stage, I continued to support the implementation through:

Developer feasibility discussions

Developer questions and clarification

BA demos

Stakeholder demos

Client submission

Implementation review

The goal was not only to make the Figma design work, but to make sure the implemented workflow matched the intended behavior.

15 — Outcome

Outcome

The MAR framework was first designed for the Emergency Department and later extended to support Inpatient workflows.

It is now connected to ED + IP + OR tracker-board workflows and is live at Hospital Metropolitano.

The design established a structured way to

View medication schedules

Understand medication timing

Identify due and overdue medications

Keep PRN and Continuous medications accessible

Document administration

Support controlled-substance verification

Review administration history

Connect medication administration with the wider EHR workflow

There is no reliable quantitative metric available for this portfolio case study, so I am not claiming a percentage improvement in efficiency or error reduction.

Core UX contribution

Translated a complex medication-administration workflow into a time-based interaction model with clear attention hierarchy, accessible medication states, and an administration workflow that fits into the wider EHR system.

16 — Reflection

Reflection

The hardest part of MAR was not designing the medication card. It was managing the different levels of attention, behavior, and system impact without making the screen difficult to use.

The key lesson for me was

In a clinical product, simplifying the interface does not always mean removing information. Sometimes it means deciding what the user should notice first.

The project also reinforced another lesson

A good screen can still fail if it does not fit the larger system.

For MAR, every design decision had an effect somewhere else in the EHR. Understanding the full workflow helped me make those decisions with more confidence.
